Seven years ago, during my years as a master student at TUM, there was a fellow, actually a physicist, who told me about Reynolds' transport theorem, which essentially is differentiation under the integral in case of varying integration domain. At that time, I recall that eventually I wanted to write up a nice simple proof of that theorem in differential geometric language. Now, 7 years later, finally the time came. Below, I also give a proof of the classical statement.

## the assertion

Let $\omega_t$ be a time-dependent $n$-form. Let $U_t$ be a time-dependent $n$-dimensional submanifold with boundary, in the sense that there is a time-dependent vector field $X_t$ such that $U_t = \varphi^t_0(U_0)$. Here $ \varphi^t_s $ denotes the non-autonomous flow of $X_{t + s}$, such that $ \varphi^{0}_{s} = \text{id} $ and $ t \mapsto \varphi^t_s(p) $ gives an integral curve of $X_{t + s}$ through $p$ for $t=0$. Then Reynolds transport theorem asserts

$$
\begin{aligned}

\frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} \int_{U_t} \omega_t &=  \int_{U_\tau} \L_{X_{\tau}} \omega_\tau + \int_{U_\tau} \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} \omega_t \\
 &= \int_{U_\tau} i_{X_{\tau}} \d \omega_\tau + \int_{\partial U_\tau} i_{X_{\tau}} \omega_\tau + \int_{U_\tau} \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} \omega_t,

\end{aligned}
\tag{*}\label{reynolds}
$$

where $\L_{X_\tau}$ denotes the Lie derivative with respect to the non-autonomous vector field $X_\tau$.

### the proof

It turns out that Reynolds transport theorem essentially amounts to change of variables combined with Stokes' theorem and another magic ingredient :) First, we do a change of variables

$$
\int_{U_t} \omega_t = \int_{\varphi^t_0(U_0)} \omega_t = \int_{U_0} (\varphi^t_0)^\ast \omega_t.
$$

Then, assuming smoothness and suitable integrability, we use dominated convergence to differentiate under the integral sign

$$
\frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} \int_{U_0} (\varphi^t_0)^\ast \omega_t = \int_{U_0} \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} (\varphi^t_0)^\ast \omega_t.
$$

The integrand looks almost like a Lie derivative. As usual, we have

$$
\frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} (\varphi^t_0)^\ast \omega_t = (\varphi^\tau_0)^\ast \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = 0} (\varphi^t_\tau)^\ast \omega_{t + \tau}
$$

Define $T\colon (r,s) \mapsto (\varphi^r_\tau)^\ast \omega_{s + \tau}$. A simple calculation shows that 

$$

\partial_r T(0,0) = \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\eval_{r=0, s=0} (\varphi^r_\tau)^\ast \omega_{s + \tau} = \L_{X_{\tau}} \omega_\tau

$$

and

$$

\partial_s T(0,0) = (\varphi^0_\tau)^\ast \frac{\d}{\d s}\eval_{s = 0} \omega_{s + \tau} = \frac{\d}{\d s}\eval_{s = \tau} \omega_{s}.

$$

By using the chain rule, we finally get our Lie derivative plus the time derivative of the $n$-form

$$

\frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = 0} (\varphi^t_\tau)^\ast \omega_{t + \tau} = \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = 0} T(t,t) = \L_{X_{\tau}} \omega_\tau  +  \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} \omega_{t}.

$$

We can establish

$$

\begin{aligned}

\frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau}  \int_{U_t} \omega_t &= \int_{U_0} (\varphi^\tau_0)^\ast \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = 0} (\varphi^t_\tau)^\ast \omega_{t + \tau}\\
 &= \int_{U_0} (\varphi^\tau_0)^\ast \left( \L_{X_{\tau}} \omega_\tau  +  \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} \omega_{t} \right) \\
 &= \int_{U_\tau} \L_{X_{t + \tau}} \omega_\tau  + \int_{U_\tau} \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} \omega_{t}.

\end{aligned}

$$

Now Cartan's magic formula is calling :) 

$$

\int_{U_\tau} \L_{X_{\tau}} \omega_\tau  =  \int_{U_\tau} i_{X_{\tau}} \d \omega_\tau + \int_{U_\tau} \d i_{X_{\tau}} \omega_\tau

$$

and a final application of Stokes' theorem to the latter summand gives \eqref{reynolds}.

## classical formulation

Differential forms are not everyones taste. So here comes the classical euclidean formulation. Let $f(t,x)$ be a smooth function and, as above, let $U_t$ be a time-dependent sufficiently regular subset, such that there is a time-dependent vector field $X_t$ such that $U_t = \varphi^t_0(U_0)$. Then Reynolds transport theorem reads

$$
\begin{aligned}

\frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} \int_{U_t} f(t,x) \d x &=  \int_{U_\tau} \nabla_x f(\tau,x) \cdot X_\tau(x) + \textrm{div}(f(\tau,x) X_\tau(x)) \d x + \int_{U_\tau} \partial_t f(\tau,x) \d x \\
 &= \int_{U_\tau} \nabla_x f(\tau,x) \cdot X_\tau(x) + \int_{\partial U_\tau} f(\tau,x) X_\tau(x) \cdot \nu(x) \d S(x) + \int_{U_\tau} \partial_t f(\tau,x) \d x

\end{aligned}
\tag{**}\label{reynolds2}
$$

### proof of classical formulation

Let's prove \eqref{reynolds2}. The strategy is the same as above, the notation just becomes a bit more messy. First, we transform the integral

$$
\int_{U_t} f(t,x) \d x = \int_{\varphi^t_0(U_0)} f(t,x) \d x = \int_{U_0} f(t,\varphi^t_0(y)) \abs{ \det \d \varphi^t_0(y) } \d y.
$$

Assuming sufficient regularity, we pull the derivative into the integral and obtain

$$
\frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} \int_{U_0} f(t,\varphi^t_0(y)) \abs{ \det \d \varphi^t_0(y) } \d y = \int_{U_0} \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} \left( f(t,\varphi^t_0(y)) \abs{ \det \d \varphi^t_0(y) } \right) \d y.
$$

We will employ the product rule. Thus, we compute by the chain rule

$$
\frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t} f(t,\varphi^t_0(y)) = \begin{bmatrix} \partial_t f(t,\varphi^t_0(y)) \\ \nabla_x f(t,\varphi^t_0(y)) \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ X_t(\varphi^t_0(y)) \end{bmatrix}
$$

and again by chain rule and using the derivative of a determinant

$$
\frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t} \det \d \varphi^t_0(y) = \det \d \varphi^t_0(y) \tr \left( (\d \varphi^t_0(y))^{-1} \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t} \d \varphi^t_0(y) \right).
$$

Note that $\frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t} \d \varphi^t_0(y) = \d \left( \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t} \varphi^t_0(y) \right) = \d ( X_t(\varphi^t_0(\cdot)) )\eval_y = D X_t(\varphi^t_0(y)) \d \varphi^t_0(y) $ and therefore 
$$
\begin{aligned}
\tr \left( (\d \varphi^t_0(y))^{-1} \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t} \d \varphi^t_0(y) \right) &= \tr \left( (\d \varphi^t_0(y))^{-1} D X_t(\varphi^t_0(y)) \d \varphi^t_0(y) \right) = \tr D X_t(\varphi^t_0(y)) \\ &= \div X_t(\varphi^t_0(y))
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} \int_{U_t} f(t,x) \d x &= \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} \int_{U_0} f(t,\varphi^t_0(y)) \abs{ \det \d \varphi^t_0(y) } \d y = \int_{U_0} \frac{\d}{\d t}\eval_{t = \tau} \left( f(t,\varphi^t_0(y)) \abs{ \det \d \varphi^t_0(y) } \right) \d y\\
	&= \int_{U_0} \partial_t f(\tau, \varphi^\tau_0(y)) + \nabla_x f(\tau,\varphi^\tau_0(y)) \cdot X_\tau (\varphi^\tau_0(y)) + f(\tau, \varphi^\tau_0(y) \div X_\tau(\varphi^\tau_0(y)) \d y\\
	&= \int_{U_\tau} \nabla_x f(\tau,x) \cdot X_\tau(x) \d x + \int_{U_\tau} \div(f(\tau,x) X_\tau(x)) \d x + \int_{U_\tau} \partial_t f(\tau,x) \d x
\end{aligned}
$$

and application of the divergence theorem to the middle integral in the latter line gives

$$
\int_{U_\tau} \div(f(\tau,x) X_\tau(x)) \d x = \int_{\partial U_\tau} f(\tau,x) X_\tau(x) \cdot \nu(x) \d S
$$

where $\nu(x)$ is an outer unit normal field of the boundary. This finishes the proof of \eqref{reynolds2}.

## comparing terms

Finally, in the euclidean setting, we can compare the terms of \eqref{reynolds} with \eqref{reynolds2}. 

The difference to \eqref{reynolds} is that we essentially have the integral 

$$ 
\int_{U_\tau} \L_{X_{\tau}} \omega_\tau = \int_{U_\tau} i_{X_{\tau}} \d \omega_\tau + \int_{U_\tau} \d i_{X_{\tau}} \omega_\tau.
$$ 

In euclidean coordinates we can write $\omega_t = f(t,\cdot) \d x_1 \wedge \dots \wedge \d x_n$. Then $\d \omega_t = \d f(t,\cdot) \wedge \d x_1 \wedge \dots \wedge \d x_n$, where $\d f(t, \cdot) = \sum_i \partial_{x_i} f(t,\cdot) \d x^{i}$. Thus we see that $i_{X_{\tau}} \d \omega_\tau = \d f(\tau,\cdot) \wedge \d x_1 \wedge \dots \wedge \d x_n (X_\tau, \cdots) = \sum_i \partial_{x_i} f(\tau, \cdot) X_\tau^{i}(\cdot) \d x_1 \wedge \dots \wedge \d x_n$. Hence in the euclidean case we can identify

$$
\int_{U_\tau} i_{X_{\tau}} \d \omega_\tau = \int_{U_\tau} \nabla_x f(\tau,x) \cdot X_\tau(x) \d x.
$$

Further, $\d i_{X_{\tau}} \omega_\tau = \div(f(\tau,x) X_\tau(x)) \d x^1 \wedge \dots \wedge \d x^n$.
